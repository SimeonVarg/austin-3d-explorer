## 1. What a person sees, and why

### 1.1 Why far lights twinkle (and why near ones do not)

Light from a far lamp crosses kilometres of air whose refractive index wanders with temperature (hot asphalt, warm
walls, wind). The wavefront arrives corrugated, and the eye's small pupil catches a bright or a dim patch of it from
moment to moment. That is scintillation: the same physics that twinkles stars, and it is stronger for city lights
seen along the ground because the path is low and turbulent.

* **Strength.** For weak turbulence the normalised intensity variance is the Rytov variance,
  `sigma_R^2 = 1.23 * Cn2 * k^(7/6) * L^(11/6)` for a plane wave, about 0.4 of that for a point source (spherical
  wave). It grows with path length as `L^(11/6)`, and with wavelength as `lambda^(-7/6)`, so blue twinkles more than red.
  Near-ground night values of the structure constant `Cn2` are of the order 1e-16 to 1e-14 m^(-2/3), and vary a lot by
  site and weather; there is no universal urban figure. At 550 nm and `Cn2 = 1e-15` the variance is about 0.007 at
  500 m and 0.025 at 1 km (fractional RMS 8% and 16%). Beyond a kilometre or two the weak-turbulence formula is
  extrapolated and then saturates. (Astra's arithmetic, re-derived: Andrews and Phillips, *Laser Beam Propagation
  through Random Media*, SPIE Press 2005.)
* **Aperture averaging.** What matters is the receiver against the Fresnel scale `sqrt(lambda * L)`: about 2 cm at 1
  km. The dark-adapted pupil is 3 to 7 mm, smaller than that, so the eye averages almost nothing and sees the full
  twinkle. A telescope averages, which is why a star twinkles less in binoculars. [Aperture averaging and
  horizontal-path measurements: a 9 cm aperture measured indices 0.66 to 1.03, a 60 cm one 0.07 to 1.08; the
  receiving-aperture and correlation-width relations are in the Rytov literature (see the search sources below).]
* **Source size averages it too, and this matters for a city.** A source wider than the angle `sqrt(lambda/L)` is a
  sum of many independent point sources and the twinkle averages out. A lamp head is a few centimetres to half a metre
  across; a window is one to two metres. At 1 km the coherence angle is about 20 micro-radians (4 arc-seconds); a 1.5
  m window subtends 1,500 micro-radians, so it averages some 75 patches and its intensity scintillation falls by
  roughly `sqrt(75)`, to a percent or two. **So the honest physics is: compact sources (lamps, LED signs, aircraft
  beacons, bare bulbs) twinkle; windows barely do.** What a person sees on a far window is a different set of
  things: image boil and wander (refraction by large eddies, which moves the light rather than changing its
  brightness), and retinal noise near the visibility threshold, where a dim light is seen on one glance and missed on
  the next because rods count only a handful of photons per integration time. The build therefore gives lamps the
  larger amplitude and windows a small one, and says so in its names.
* **Time scale.** The Fresnel scale crossed by a 1 to 10 m/s wind gives characteristic rates of tens to hundreds of
  hertz, far above anything the eye follows. What a person perceives is the slow part: the rod pathway is sluggish
  (time constants about 200 ms) and flicker fusion at low light is in the mid-teens of hertz at best (Ferry-Porter:
  critical flicker frequency rises with the log of luminance; rod-mediated figures are lower than cone ones). So the
  visible twinkle is a few hertz, and the build uses two slow oscillators per light between 2.2 and 6.8 Hz, which a 15
  Hz redraw can show without aliasing.
* **Why near lights do not.** The path is short (`L^(11/6)`), so the variance is negligible, and a near window
  subtends a large angle. Nothing within 250 m twinkles in the build, ramping to full at 2 km.

Sources: [scintillation index and the Rytov variance on horizontal paths](https://www.radioeng.cz/fulltexts/2009/09_01_042_047.pdf);
[aperture averaging, correlation width](https://www.astro.auth.gr/~seeing-gr/seeing_gr_files/theory/node29.html);
[Why stars and city lights twinkle (popular)](https://thehobartmagazine.com.au/why-do-city-lights-and-stars-twinkle/);
[rod time constants and flicker, Webvision](https://webvision.med.utah.edu/?p=159).

### 1.2 The eye at night

* **Three regimes.** Photopic (cones) above about 3 cd/m2; mesopic (both) roughly 0.005 to 5 cd/m2 (CIE 191:2010
  puts the mesopic system at about 0.005 to 5); scotopic (rods) below. The street of a lit city at night sits in the
  mesopic range, the dark side of a hill in the scotopic one.
* **Purkinje shift.** The rod peak is near 507 nm against 555 nm for cones, so as light drops the blues and greens gain
  relative brightness and reds lose it: blue things are brighter at night than red things of the same daytime
  brightness. The CIE mesopic luminous efficiency is a blend `V_mes = X*V + (1-X)*V'` with X running from 0
  (scotopic) to 1 (photopic); at 0.1 cd/m2 the two published weightings give X of 0.28 and 0.42, at 1 cd/m2 1.0 and
  0.70. Colour desaturates and drifts toward a dull blue-violet; acuity falls with log luminance; noise becomes
  visible.
* **What this means for a renderer.** Do **not** make every far thing blue. A bright distant lamp keeps its colour:
  the shift applies to the *adapted* surroundings, not to distance. The scene's dim surfaces take the blue-green
  tint (this city's unlit walls already read blue-grey), the lit points stay warm, and the contrast of warm lights
  against a cool dark is itself what a person reports as "the colour of a night city". The build adds no
  distance-based blue shift. It does give the eye's glare a cooler tint (below) because scattered light in the media
  of the eye is bluer than the source.
* **Dark adaptation** takes tens of minutes for the rods; a game or a city viewer cannot use it without making the
  user wait, and the auto-exposure in `js/graphics.js` already stands in for it.

Sources: [Purkinje effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purkinje_effect), [mesopic vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopic_vision).

### 1.3 Glare and veiling luminance

A point of light is not a point on the retina. Scatter in the cornea, lens and retina and diffraction at the lens
fibres give every bright point a *point spread function* (PSF) with a bright narrow core, a steep power-law skirt
("bloom"), a ring or halo (the lenticular halo, a few degrees across, wavelength-dependent), and the radial spikes of
the ciliary corona. Spencer, Shirley, Zimmerman and Greenberg, ["Physically-based glare effects for digital
images", SIGGRAPH 1995, pp. 325-334](https://w1.graphics.cornell.edu/pubs/1995/SSZG95.pdf), build a digital PSF from
the psychophysics, one each for photopic, mesopic and scotopic adaptation, and show that convolving an image with it
raises the *apparent* brightness of light sources (a perceptual test in the paper). Rendering glare is a convolution;
games approximate it with a stack of blurs.

* **Practical shape.** A narrow core, power-law wings, and a halo near 3 degrees. Astra's three-Gaussian
  approximation (weights 0.85 / 0.12 / 0.03, standard deviations 0.03 / 0.2 / 1 degree) is, by its own statement, a
  heuristic and not Spencer's coefficients. I did not retrieve the paper's exact terms this session, so the build uses
  the *structure* (tight, middle, wide) with named widths and weights, not the published constants.
* **Veiling luminance.** The Stiles-Holladay disability glare equation: `L_v = 10 * E / theta^2` (cd/m2, `E` in lux at
  the eye, `theta` in degrees, valid roughly 1 to 30 degrees; CIE 135/146 give the age-corrected and wide-angle
  forms, `k = 9.86 * [1 + (A/66.4)^4]` for the European road-lighting form). It is a *veil over the whole field*, not
  a normalised PSF. For a renderer it says the wide skirt is small close in and falls as the inverse square: the
  middle and wide lobes in the build are weighted that way (a fifth and a sixth of the tight bloom's strength).
* **Not physically motivated:** a uniform full-frame bloom at constant radius. The existing bloom in `graphics.js`
  thresholds the frame (`brightness * contrast(4)`) and blurs at about 0.2 degrees, which is the *core* of the
  glare; the build adds the other two lobes beside it.

Sources: [Spencer et al. 1995 bibliography and abstract](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=118751), [Stanford rendering-glare
implementation notes](https://graphics.stanford.edu/courses/cs348b-competition/cs348b-03/glare/), [glare basics,
LRC](https://www.lrc.rpi.edu/programs/Transportation/pdf/glareBasics.pdf), [CIE 135 and 146](https://cie.co.at/node/1265).

### 1.4 Colours of the real sources

* **Street lamps.** High-pressure sodium is about 2000 to 2200 K and a distinctive amber with almost no blue; white
  LEDs are 3000 to 4000 K. Austin Energy replaced about 35,000 sodium fixtures with LEDs; the vendor press release
  names 4000 K standard with 3000 K and 6000 K options, and Austin's dark-sky stance favours the warmer end; Knoxville
  and others chose 3000 K. This repository's streetlights already follow the Austin 3000 K story (`js/night.js`,
  "warm everywhere"); the build leaves them alone.
* **Interiors.** Incandescent about 2700 K; residential LED commonly 2700 to 3000 K; offices 4000 to 5000 K
  (fluorescent or LED); a TV lights a room with a flickering blue-white. The generic building atlas (`facades.js`
  `WINDOW_TONES`) already has warm incandescent, warm LED, neutral, cool office and a 3% TV blue, with family warm
  biases. The authored buildings (`city-night.js`) had four tones for everything; the build gives offices a
  cool-white palette and homes a warm one with 4.5% TV-blue rooms (`?nightcolour=0` puts the four tones back).
* **Signs.** Coloured, saturated, and the brightest things in a night street; the repository's sign layer already marks
  luminous signs.

### 1.5 How many windows are lit, by hour and type

I could not find a published hourly occupancy curve for apartments or offices. What exists:

* A WPI project report on non-domestic buildings counted lit windows street-side: its excerpt cites **16.6%** of
  windows lit on weekend evenings 7 to 10 pm and **18.9%** on weeknight surveys in an earlier (2007) study, and it
  extended its own sampling to 10 pm to 2 am and 2 to 5 am, treating the 2 to 5 am level as constant (so offices stay
  at a floor all night, cleaners and security). [WPI report, 2008 PDF](https://digital.wpi.edu/downloads/wp988k55s?locale=it).
* Van Doren et al., PNAS 2021, show a single convention centre with half its windows dark killed 11 times fewer
  migrating birds in spring: building window-lighting is a controllable, large-scale quantity, but the paper gives no
  transferable occupancy schedule. [summary](https://news.umich.edu/turning-off-lights-can-save-migrating-birds-from-crashing-into-buildings).
* A Krakow study (Sciezor) notes that in the large majority of cases apartment lighting is off before midnight.
* Astra's labelled *starting priors* (no source; calibrate locally): apartments 50% / 25% / 10% lit at 20:00 /
  00:00 / 03:00, offices 25% / 10% / 5%.

The build does not invent a curve from these. It keeps the existing per-building occupancy (hash-picked, unchanged)
as the *evening* state and lets windows go dark in order as the slider moves toward late night: each lit window has a
fixed bedtime (a hash of its colour name and a 37 m by 37 m by 11 m cell); the share that has gone dark at the end of
the slider is 18% for homes, and 41% for offices (`1 + officeExtra 1.3` times as many), set by `lateDropout` and
`officeExtra`. That is a calibration knob, not a measurement; it ends well above the 3 am priors on purpose, because
this is a *picture* and a city that is half dark reads as a power cut.

### 1.6 Temporal behaviour

Lights change by the minute: a window comes on when someone gets home and goes off when they go to bed; TVs flicker
within seconds; traffic is continuous. The build covers the slow part with a private 150 to 900 s cycle per lit
window in which it rests 4.5% of the time (`switchS`, `offBase`), plus the late-night dropout above. Traffic and
TV flicker are not in the build.

### 1.7 What film and game teams do

* **Interior mapping** (van Dongen, *Interior Mapping: A new technique for rendering realistic buildings*, CGI 2008)
  and its "fake interior" cousins draw a room behind each window from a texture warped with the view angle; Insomniac's
  Spider-Man windows look like this, though the studio has not documented it ([80.lv write-up](https://80.lv/articles/interior-mapping-rendering-real-rooms-without-geometry),
  [Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/news/20231201-23558/)). It earns its keep at street level; at the
  distances the shimmer lives at, a window is a coloured dot.
* **Cyberpunk 2077** (GDC 2022, "Bringing Light to Night City", Knapik, Yablonsky, Poo): a "living digital film set",
  a fully dynamic 24-hour cycle because the city could not be baked, and the unprecedented density of lights as the
  headache ([session page](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/find-out-how-cyberpunk-2077-lit-up-night-city-in-this-gdc-2022-session)).
  I could not read the talk itself, so I claim nothing about its emissive-window method.
* **Common practice** (from Astra, unverified for any specific title): emissive atlases with stable per-window
  variation, bloom, and distance LOD that collapses far windows to points. This build is that, plus shimmer.

## 2. What was built

Everything is behind a named value in `CityNight.eye` (`js/city-night.js`) and a URL switch. `?nighteye=0` puts the
previous night back whole.

| Part | Where | Default | Switch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Far-light shimmer, windows | `cityEyeGain` in `js/city-lighting.js` (MapLibre buildings) and `js/slopes.js` (authored buildings) | 14% RMS from 2 km, none inside 250 m | `?twinkle=<n>` (0 off, 2 double) |
| Far-light shimmer, lamp heads | circle vertex shader patch in `js/city-lighting.js` | head radius breathes 30% RMS, same distance ramp | `?twinkle` |
| Glare lobes | bloom pass in `js/graphics.js` | middle (96 px canvas, about 0.9 deg), wide (40 px, about 2 deg), cool tint | `?glare=<n>` |
| Colour by building type | `pickTone` in `js/city-night.js` | homes warm + 4.5% TV blue, offices cool white | `?nightcolour=0` |
| Slow change | `cityEyeGain` | 4.5% of a private 150-900 s cycle at rest; late-night dropout 18% homes / 41% offices | `?nightdrift=0` |
| Frozen night | `CityNight.now()/rand()`, `js/sky.js`, `js/graphics.js` | off | `?nightfreeze=1`, `?nightseed=<n>` |
| Repaint for a parked camera | ticker in `js/city-night.js` | 15 Hz, graphics-card tiers only, stops after 5 idle minutes | `?twinkle=1` forces it on |

### Design decisions worth knowing

1. **A window names itself by its colour.** The room palette gives every lit window its own colour (tone times a
   per-window brightness), so hashing the 8-bit RGB of the lit texel names the window with no geometry and no new
   data. The same atlas tile repeats on many walls, so a position-linear phase (about 0.09 rad/m) separates walls.
2. **Windows are aperture-averaged on screen too.** The shimmer fades out where one pixel covers more than 3 to 12 m
   of wall (`footprintM`), so far pixels that hold many windows do not boil with aliased noise.
3. **No per-frame CPU work.** The shimmer is a function of `(window name, seconds)` evaluated in the fragment shader.
   JavaScript writes two uniforms per frame.
4. **A parked camera needs frames.** This app draws only when something changes, so nothing would ever move. A 15 Hz
   ticker redraws a parked night camera while shimmer is on, the tab is visible, the camera is still and someone
   touched the page in the last five minutes; it never runs on phones or integrated chips unless `?twinkle=1`.
5. **The frozen night.** `?nightfreeze=1` holds the night clock at one instant and seeds the random numbers.
   Everything time-driven in the night reads `CityNight.now()` (the star field's twinkle and drift in `sky.js`, the
   shimmer, the slow switching) and the film-grain tile reads the seeded generator. The picture checks append it
   (`LOOK.shotQuery`). See the determinism result below.

## 4. Not done, and why

* Traffic, TV flicker and moving lights.
* Angular image boil (refractive wander of far lights): needs a per-fragment displacement of the *whole* frame; a
  full-screen pass, which this build refuses on the low tiers.
* The ciliary corona's radial spikes (directional glare): the lobes are isotropic. Spikes need a star filter on the
  brightest points only.
* A per-light halo that grows with brightness for lamps individually; the bloom's threshold already grows a halo with
  brightness, and the lamp pools are ground-lights.
* Published hourly occupancy: none found; the late-night dropout is a taste value.
